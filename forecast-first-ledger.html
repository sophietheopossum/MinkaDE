Build brief · personal finance

Forecast-First Ledger

Eight requirements, a double-entry core, and the prior art worth stealing from. The stated features are the cheap half — this brief is mostly about the half that isn't stated.

Prepared 28 Aug 2026 Status Pre-decision Benchmark PocketSmith, $120/yr

Where this came from

A survey of existing tools against eight requirements. PocketSmith was the only product that met all eight; Firefly III fails on forecasting as an explicit design decision, and Actual Budget has no scenario modelling to build on.

The question that produced this document was whether to build instead. The short answer is that it's more viable than it first looks, for a non-obvious reason: the eight requirements below happen to be the part that's expensive to buy and cheap to build. The genuinely hard parts of a finance app aren't in the list, because they're invisible until you're in it.

The eight requirements

Build cost assumes a double-entry core and a pure-function forecast. Criteria 2, 4 and 8 are the ones no free tool does well — and, not coincidentally, the ones with the best published prior art.

# Requirement Cost Prior art to lift
1 Recurring payments, in and out Low RFC 5545 RRULE; hledger periodic rules
2 Forecast those recurrences Medium hledger --forecast generated transactions
3 Sudden one-off payments Trivial Falls out of the schema — a txn with no series
4 Alter one occurrence's date and amount Medium RFC 5545 RECURRENCE-ID / EXDATE; GnuCash Since Last Run
5 Trace money across accounts and services Low Firefly III bidirectional transaction links
6 Store payment history Trivial It's the table. Nothing to design.
7 Export for analysis by an LLM Trivial SQLite file + SELECT; Beancount's query layer
8 Simulate a new recurring payment Low PocketSmith secondary scenarios

If the forecast is a pure function over rules, a what-if scenario is an extra rule set passed in and toggled. The expensive commercial feature becomes a boolean parameter.

The core architectural bet

What actually costs you

None of this was in the requirements. All of it is where the real effort goes, and the reason most self-built finance tools are abandoned around month three.

Concern Cost Why it bites
Bank feeds / ingestion High + ongoing Plaid, TrueLayer, GoCardless Bank Account Data, Salt Edge, Akahu. PSD2 forces consent re-authentication roughly every 90 days, so even a working feed needs a re-auth flow you build and maintain. Viable-to-free under UK/EU open banking; materially worse in the US.
Transaction matching High Reconciling an imported real transaction against the occurrence you forecast. Fuzzy and endless. Actual Budget's "within 2 days before or after" window is a heuristic born of pain — expect to rediscover all of it.
Durability over years Ongoing The stated goal was something dependable. Backups, migrations that don't eat history, and — the actual risk — still caring in month 30. A finance tool only pays off across years of sustained entry.
Multi-currency High, if needed Historical rates, realised vs unrealised gain, which rate applies at posting time. Nasty to retrofit. Decide before the schema is written, not after.
Entry on a phone Medium Logging a payment while standing in a shop. Most often the thing that quietly kills adoption of a self-built tool.

Prior art worth reading

Ordered by how directly each one solves a stated requirement. The first two are the highest-leverage: both are specifications or implementations of exactly the problems that looked hardest.

Criteria 1 & 4 · specification

RFC 5545 (iCalendar)

The recurrence problem was solved and standardised in 2009. RRULE defines the pattern, RDATE adds one-off occurrences, EXDATE removes them, and RECURRENCE-ID exists specifically to identify and override a single occurrence within a series.

StealRequirement 4 is RECURRENCE-ID under another name. Don't invent an override model — implement this one. Use python-dateutil's rrule or rrule.js rather than hand-rolled date maths.

Criteria 1, 2 & 8 · implementation

hledger forecasting

Periodic transaction rules live in the journal; the --forecast flag activates them to generate temporary transactions that are never written back but appear in every report. Inspect them with hledger print --forecast tag:generated.

StealThe whole architecture. Forecast output is derived and disposable, not persisted rows. That single decision is what makes scenarios cheap and keeps projections from corrupting the ledger.

Criterion 8 · interaction model

PocketSmith scenarios

Each account has a primary scenario — the real forecast — and optional secondary scenarios that are additive/subtractive overlays. Tick and untick them on the calendar to recompute projected daily balances with and without.

StealThe overlay-plus-toggle model, and the comparison against a live baseline rather than a separate throwaway budget. Also its occurrence editing: this event only / this and future / all.

Criterion 5 · data model

Firefly III

Open source, mature double-entry schema, and the best answer anywhere to tracing money across services: explicit typed links between transactions that work in both directions, configurable under Administration.

StealThe link table and its typed, bidirectional relations. Also read its Missing features page — a rare, explicit statement of what a finance tool deliberately refuses to do, and why forecasting was ruled out.

Reference implementation

Actual Budget

The most readable open-source codebase of the group, and the budget file is plain SQLite. Its schedules implementation covers requirement 1 directly, and its local-first sync layer is worth a look even if you never need it.

StealThe schema, and the matching heuristics as a cautionary tale. Its two-day matching window shows how much fuzziness reconciliation actually needs.

Criterion 4 · interaction model

GnuCash "Since Last Run"

The canonical UX for scheduled transactions: on opening the file, you're shown every occurrence that came due and can review, edit or skip each one before it posts to the ledger. Amounts can be variables it prompts you for.

StealThe review-before-commit step. It resolves requirement 4 without any editing-history complexity, because nothing has been written yet.

Criteria 5 & 7 · data model

Beancount & Fava

Plain-text double-entry with a rigorously designed data model and a real query language. Fava is its web frontend. The whole system treats the ledger as queryable data first and a UI second.

StealThe posting model and the discipline of a text-first, queryable ledger. This is the shape that makes requirement 7 — handing clean data to an LLM — free rather than a feature.

Architecture decisions

Five calls that are cheap now and expensive to reverse. The first is the one that matters most.

Double-entry, day one
Every transaction is a set of postings summing to zero. Retrofitting this is a rewrite. It's also what makes requirement 5 fall out for free — and it removes PocketSmith's two-accounts-per-transfer limit, so a multi-hop journey is genuinely representable.
SQLite, single file
Hands you requirement 7 and a backup story in the same decision. One file to copy, and any LLM pipeline can read it directly.
Integer minor units
Amounts as signed integers in pence/cents. Never floats. Non-negotiable and unglamorous.
Forecast as a pure function
No I/O, no writes, no persisted projection rows. Testable, and it's what collapses requirement 8 into a parameter.
CSV import before any bank API
Prove the model against real history first. Feeds are a separate project with a separate maintenance burden, and may never be worth starting.

Schema sketch

Enough to argue with. The UNIQUE (series_id, occurrence_on) constraint is doing quiet but important work — it's what stops a forecast occurrence and its real imported counterpart from both landing in the ledger.

-- Core. A txn is a set of postings summing to zero.
CREATE TABLE account (
  id            INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
  name          TEXT NOT NULL,
  kind          TEXT NOT NULL,   -- asset|liability|income|expense|equity
  currency      TEXT NOT NULL
);

CREATE TABLE txn (
  id            INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
  occurred_on   TEXT NOT NULL,            -- ISO date
  description   TEXT NOT NULL,
  series_id     INTEGER REFERENCES series(id),   -- null = one-off (req 3)
  occurrence_on TEXT,                     -- RECURRENCE-ID: the slot filled
  journey_id    INTEGER REFERENCES journey(id),  -- req 5
  source        TEXT NOT NULL,            -- manual|import
  UNIQUE (series_id, occurrence_on)
);

CREATE TABLE posting (
  id            INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
  txn_id        INTEGER NOT NULL REFERENCES txn(id) ON DELETE CASCADE,
  account_id    INTEGER NOT NULL REFERENCES account(id),
  amount_minor  INTEGER NOT NULL,         -- signed. integers only.
  currency      TEXT NOT NULL
);
-- invariant: SUM(amount_minor) = 0 per txn, per currency

-- Req 1: store the rule, never the instances.
CREATE TABLE series (
  id            INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
  description   TEXT NOT NULL,
  rrule         TEXT NOT NULL,            -- RFC 5545 RRULE string
  dtstart       TEXT NOT NULL,
  until_on      TEXT,
  template      TEXT NOT NULL,            -- JSON: default postings
  scenario_id   INTEGER REFERENCES scenario(id)  -- null = baseline (req 8)
);

-- Req 4: per-occurrence override. RECURRENCE-ID by another name.
CREATE TABLE series_override (
  series_id     INTEGER NOT NULL REFERENCES series(id) ON DELETE CASCADE,
  occurrence_on TEXT NOT NULL,            -- the slot being overridden
  moved_to      TEXT,                     -- new date, or null to keep
  amount_minor  INTEGER,                  -- new amount, or null to keep
  skipped       INTEGER NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,     -- EXDATE
  PRIMARY KEY (series_id, occurrence_on)
);

-- Req 8: a scenario is just a named bag of extra series.
CREATE TABLE scenario (
  id            INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
  name          TEXT NOT NULL,
  active        INTEGER NOT NULL DEFAULT 0
);

-- Req 5: one movement of money, many hops.
CREATE TABLE journey (
  id            INTEGER PRIMARY KEY,
  label         TEXT NOT NULL
);

The forecast function

forecast(as_of, horizon, active_scenarios[])
    -> [{ account, date, balance }]

  1. seed balances from real postings up to as_of
  2. for each series where
       scenario_id IS NULL OR scenario_id IN active_scenarios:
         expand rrule from as_of to horizon
         apply overrides  (skip / move / re-amount)
         drop occurrences already matched by a real txn
  3. accumulate forward by date, per account

Generated occurrences are never written back. Requirement 2 is step 3; requirement 8 is the third argument.

Open questions

Four answers that change the build materially. The first one decides most of it.

Which country do the accounts sit in? Decides whether automatic bank feeds are free, expensive, or unavailable — and therefore whether building is a weekend project or an ongoing integration commitment. UK/EU open banking makes DIY feeds viable; elsewhere, plan for manual CSV indefinitely.
Is multi-currency actually needed? PayPal and Wise in the money path usually means yes. It has to be settled before the schema is written — historical rates and gain/loss are miserable to retrofit.
Does entry need to work on a phone? If yes, that's a hosting, auth and mobile-UI project sitting on top of the ledger, and it should be scoped as one.
A working system, or a project? Both are legitimate, and they have different answers. Building purely to avoid $120/yr doesn't survive the arithmetic once maintenance is counted; wanting to own the tool does. Worth being honest about which is driving it before committing six months.

The middle path

Worth putting on the table explicitly, because it's likely the best value for effort: don't build ingestion, build the analysis.

Let an existing tool be the system of record and own bank feeds, matching and durability — Actual Budget's SQLite file, Firefly III's REST API, or PocketSmith's own API and CSV export. Then build the forecast-and-scenario layer on top as a script against that data.

That gets requirements 2 and 8 shaped exactly as wanted without ever owning bank connectivity. And because the forecast code reads a table of transactions — which every one of these exports — it survives a migration if the tool underneath disappoints.
